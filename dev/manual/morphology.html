Morphology ​

The sizes of body surfaces and organs determine how organisms exchange heat and water with their environment, and how their bodies are built.

Surface areas ​

Heat and water are exchanged across the body surface. If shape does not change with size, surface area scales with mass to the power 2/3, so that small animals have more surface per unit mass. surface_area gives this isometric baseline for mammals (Schmidt-Nielsen 1975):

julia
surface_area(EutherianMammal(), 70.0u"kg")
1.7009063115667993 m^2

For insulated animals, the area of the skin and the area of the outer surface of the fur or feathers differ. skin_area and plumage_area give them for birds and mammals (Walsberg and King 1978):

julia
skin_area(PasserineBird(), 20.0u"g")
73.75424253432696 cm^2

Silhouette areas ​

The radiation an animal absorbs from the sun depends on its silhouette area, the area of its shadow on a plane perpendicular to the sun's rays. This depends on the orientation of the animal to the sun: the silhouette is largest when the body is side-on (normal) to the sun's rays, and smallest when it points along them (parallel). silhouette_area gives both, for reference species of lizard and frog:

The desert iguana, Dipsosaurus dorsalis (Porter et al. 1973, Porter and Tracy 1983):

julia
silhouette_area(NormalToSun(), DesertIguana(), 50.0u"g")
0.005494794733976101 m^2

By turning side-on to the sun or facing it, a lizard can change the radiation it absorbs about four-fold:

julia
masses = exp10.(range(0, 3; length = 50)) .* u"g"
fig, ax = figure_axis("Body mass (g)", "Silhouette area (cm²)"; xscale = log10, yscale = log10)
for (orientation, label) in ((NormalToSun(), "Normal to the sun"), (ParallelToSun(), "Parallel to the sun"))
    areas = silhouette_area.(Ref(orientation), Ref(DesertIguana()), masses)
    lines!(ax, ustrip.(u"g", masses), ustrip.(u"cm^2", areas); linewidth = 2, label)
end
axislegend(ax; position = :lt)
fig

Skeleton and brain ​

A larger animal needs a proportionally heavier skeleton to support its weight, so skeleton mass scales with an exponent above 1, 1.13 for mammals (Schmidt-Nielsen 1975). As a percentage of body mass:

julia
[(mass, round(100 * uconvert(NoUnits, skeleton_mass(EutherianMammal(), mass) / mass); digits = 1))
 for mass in [0.02, 2.0, 200.0, 2000.0] .* u"kg"]
4-element Vector{Tuple{Quantity{Float64, 𝐌, Unitful.FreeUnits{(kg,), 𝐌, nothing}}, Float64}}:
 (0.02 kg, 3.7)
 (2.0 kg, 6.7)
 (200.0 kg, 12.1)
 (2000.0 kg, 16.4)

Brain mass scales with an exponent below 1, and differs between mammals, primates and humans:

julia
brain_mass(EutherianMammal(), 70.0u"kg")
0.19568999764242134 kg

Body-part proportions ​

Composite bodies, such as a human made of a head, trunk, arms and legs, are described by the fraction of mass and surface area in each part, each part's shape, and the fraction of its surface that joins other parts. body_part_proportions gives these for humans, from NicheMapR:

julia
proportions = body_part_proportions(Human())
proportions.part_names, proportions.mass_fraction
([:head, :trunk, :arms, :legs], [0.07609801, 0.50069348, 0.04932963, 0.16227462])

The mass of each part of a 70 kg person is then:

julia
proportions.part_names .=> proportions.mass_fraction .* 70.0u"kg"
4-element Vector{Pair{Symbol, Quantity{Float64, 𝐌, Unitful.FreeUnits{(kg,), 𝐌, nothing}}}}:
  :head => 5.326860699999999 kg
 :trunk => 35.0485436 kg
  :arms => 3.4530741 kg
  :legs => 11.359223400000001 kg

The aspect_ratio of each part is the shape parameter of the cylinders and ellipsoids of BiophysicalGeometry.jl, which builds the composite body for heat budgets.
